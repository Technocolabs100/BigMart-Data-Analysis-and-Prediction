## Hypotheses (`reports/hypotheses.md`)

| Hypothesis | Evidence | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| Higher-priced products bring more revenue | corr(MRP, sales) = 0.57; MRP alone explains 32% of the variance | Confirmed: the strongest driver |
| Price lowers the number of units sold | corr(MRP, units) = 0.01 | Rejected: units sold do not depend on price |
| Store format drives sales | units per product: grocery 2.4, supermarkets 13.9-26.6; eta2 = 0.24 | Confirmed: grocery stores sell ~7x fewer units |
| Bigger stores sell more | High 2,299, Medium 2,682, Small 1,867; eta2 = 0.048 | Weak: the size label adds little once store type is known |
| Location tier (city size) matters | Tier 1 1,877, Tier 2 2,324, Tier 3 2,280; eta2 = 0.013 | Weak: mostly reflects which store types sit in each tier |
| Older, established stores sell more | corr(store age, sales) = 0.05; OUT027 (1985) is the best seller, OUT019 (1985) among the worst | Rejected: age is not a driver on its own |
| More shelf visibility means more sales | corr(visibility, sales) = -0.13 | Rejected: slightly negative, not positive |
| Healthy (low-fat) products sell more | units: Low Fat 15.4 vs Regular 15.6; ANOVA p = 0.29 | Rejected: no difference |
| Some product categories sell much better | units per product 14.3-16.4 across 16 types; eta2 = 0.001 | Rejected: category barely matters |

## Why every model stops near R2 = 0.60

Within one store, a product's units sold vary widely around the store's average (the
residual spread grows with the sales level, `reports/figures/residuals.png`), and no column
explains that variation: product type, fat content, weight and visibility carry almost no
signal (permutation importance below 5 RMSE units each, `reports/feature_importance.csv`).
Better predictions need new data (promotions, shelf position, local demand, stock-outs),
not a bigger model.
